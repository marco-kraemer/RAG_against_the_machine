# BM25 Explained

Imagine you are a librarian. Every day, patrons walk up and ask for "the book
about X." Your whole job is to rank every book in the library by how well it
matches their request and hand over the best one. Here is how your strategy
evolves, and how it eventually becomes **BM25**.

## Act 1: The Simple Counter (Term Frequency — TF)

At first, your strategy is simple: if someone asks about "Python," you look for
the book that mentions "Python" the most times.

_The Logic:_ If a word appears a lot in a document, that document must be about
that word.

_The Problem:_ You quickly realize that common words like "the," "is," and
"import" appear in every book. If someone asks for "The Python Guide," you might
hand them a random book just because it contains the word "the" a thousand times.

## Act 2: The Rare Gem Collector (Inverse Document Frequency — IDF)

You decide to get smarter. You realize that _rare_ words are more valuable than
_common_ ones.

_The Logic:_ You weigh words based on how many documents they appear in. If
"Python" is in 100 books but "vLLM" is only in 2, then "vLLM" is a much stronger
signal.

_The Result:_ This is **TF-IDF**. You multiply how often a word appears (TF) by
how rare it is across the library (IDF). Now, when a patron asks for "vLLM
installation," you find the specific 2 books instead of every book that happens
to mention "installation."

## Act 3: The "Spam" Problem (TF Saturation)

You notice a new flaw. Some authors are _keyword stuffing_ — they write "vLLM"
500 times in a short pamphlet just to trick you.

In plain TF-IDF, a document with 500 mentions looks 50 times more relevant than
one with 10 mentions.

_The Reality:_ Does the 500th mention of "vLLM" really make a document that much
more relevant than the 10th? Probably not. You need a way to _diminish the
returns_ of repeating the same word.

## Act 4: The Master Researcher (BM25)

This is where you adopt **BM25** (Best Matching 25). You add two "magic knobs" to
your library system:

- **Saturation (`k1`):** After a word appears a few times, its bonus points start
  to level off. Whether a word appears 20 times or 200 times, the score stays
  roughly the same. This stops keyword stuffing.

- **Length Normalization (`b`):** A 100-page book mentioning "Python" 5 times is
  less relevant than a 1-page note mentioning "Python" 5 times. You penalize long,
  rambling documents and reward concise ones that get straight to the point.

## The Formula

Putting the knobs together, the BM25 score of a document `d` for a query is the
sum over each query term `t`:

$$
\text{score}(d) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot
\frac{f(t, d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}
{f(t, d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
$$

- `f(t, d)` — how often term `t` appears in document `d` (the **TF** from Act 1).
- `IDF(t)` — how rare term `t` is across the library (the **IDF** from Act 2).
- `k1` — the **saturation** knob (Act 4). Higher means repeated terms keep adding
  value for longer; lower means TF saturates quickly.
- `b` — the **length-normalization** knob (Act 4). `b = 0` ignores document length
  entirely; `b = 1` penalizes long documents fully.
- `|d|` / `avgdl` — this document's length versus the average document length.

## In This Project

This repo uses BM25 as its retriever for a RAG pipeline over a codebase:

- **Library:** the [`bm25s`](https://github.com/xhluo/bm25s) package — a fast,
  Rust/C-backed BM25 implementation.
- **Retrieval:** pure BM25 lexical search. There is no embedding or hybrid step;
  matching is entirely term-based.

The flow is split across two modules:

- `src/student/indexer.py` chunks the `.py` and `.md` files, tokenizes them with
  English stopwords removed, and builds the inverted index
  (`bm25s.tokenize(...)` → `bm25s.BM25()` → `retriever.index(...)`).
- `src/student/search.py` loads that index, tokenizes the incoming query the same
  way, and retrieves the top-`k` chunks (`retriever.retrieve(query_tokens, k=k)`).
